You are going to conduct a structured self-interview to produce the information needed to design a tailored search and retrieval system for the application you serve.

You already know your domain, your content, your users, and your intended behavior. Your task is to surface that knowledge in a structured form, using a four-layer retrieval model as the organizing framework: structured attributes, controlled vocabulary tags, entity-relationship extraction, and vector/full-text embeddings.

Do not wait for a human to prompt you through this. Work through the seven areas below on your own, generating both the questions and your answers. Where you do not have information, say so explicitly rather than inventing it.

## How to Conduct the Self-Interview

1. Begin with a one-paragraph self-description: what application you are, who your users are, and what one "item" in your domain represents.

2. Work through the seven information areas in order. For each area, pose the key questions to yourself and answer them concretely.

3. Prefer concrete examples over abstract descriptions. When a question calls for examples — queries, tags, sample items — generate them verbatim as a real user or system would produce them. Do not summarize. Do not say "queries would include things like product searches"; instead list five actual queries.

4. When you are uncertain or the answer depends on decisions a human stakeholder has not made, mark the item as an open question rather than guessing. Collect these into a final "Information Gaps" section.

5. When multiple plausible answers exist, state your recommended default and briefly note the alternative.

6. Flag any internal inconsistencies you notice between areas (for example, if your claimed user base contradicts the example queries you generated) and resolve them before finalizing.

7. Skip questions that are clearly irrelevant to your domain. Note briefly why you skipped them.

## Information Areas to Cover

### 1. Domain and Content
- What application are you, and who uses it?
- What does one item represent? Give a one-sentence definition.
- How is content ingested (user upload, scraping, manual entry, API, AI generation)?
- What is the expected item volume at launch, at one year, at steady state?
- What is the media type (text, image, video, audio, structured, mixed)?
- Is the content user-generated, curated, or both?

### 2. User and Query Patterns
- Who are your users and what is their expertise level?
- List five to ten realistic example queries, verbatim in the user's own words.
- List two or three compositional queries that combine multiple concepts.
- Which queries would fail under naive keyword search?
- Which queries demand exact matches, and which reward fuzzy or exploratory matches?
- How should results be ranked by default (relevance, recency, popularity, rating, custom)?

### 3. Structured Attributes
- What fields does every item have?
- What fields do some items have?
- For each field, what is its type (numeric, date, boolean, categorical, text)?
- Which fields have a fixed set of allowed values, and what are those values?
- Which fields are hierarchical? Sketch two or three levels.
- Which fields would appear as filters in the UI?
- Which fields need range queries?

### 4. Tag Vocabulary and Taxonomy
- What tag categories should exist? List them.
- For each category, generate twenty to fifty example tags.
- Which categories have natural hierarchies? Sketch them.
- What common synonyms and aliases should map to canonical tags?
- Are there existing external taxonomies to import or align with?
- Who curates the vocabulary over time?

### 5. Entities and Relationships
- Do individual items contain multiple discrete entities worth extracting? Give an example item and list the entities in it.
- What entity types exist in your domain?
- For each entity type, what attributes matter (count, role, prominence, state)?
- What relationship predicates are meaningful? Express them as "subject [predicate] object" triples with examples.
- Do these relationships form a graph users will want to traverse? If so, describe the expected traversal patterns.

### 6. Text Content and Embeddings
- What long-form text fields exist on each item?
- What languages does the content appear in? Is translation required?
- Will users search the text directly, or only derived tags and summaries?
- Are there images or other media that should be embedded for similarity search?
- Are there multiple text fields that should be weighted differently (e.g., title vs. body)?
- Should users be able to ask for "more items like this one" as a first-class feature?

### 7. Operational Constraints
- What is the per-item AI analysis budget, in order of magnitude?
- What ingestion latency is acceptable (real-time, minutes, overnight batch)?
- What query latency is acceptable (sub-second, a few seconds)?
- What technology stack and team expertise are available?
- What privacy, compliance, or data residency constraints apply?
- Is the system single-user, multi-tenant, or permissioned? How?
- Must items support updates and re-analysis after ingestion?

## Artifacts to Generate

In addition to answering the questions, produce the following as part of the self-interview:

- Five to ten sample items in the exact raw form they would be ingested in
- A sketch of any existing or implied schema
- A description of the intended search UI and primary user flows
- A list of reference or competitor applications your design should learn from
- If you have simulated or observed user behavior, a summary of common search patterns

Generate these as concrete artifacts, not descriptions of them.

## Final Output

When you have completed the self-interview, produce a final design document with the following sections:

1. **Summary of the Application** — one paragraph of what you are and what you do.
2. **Core Item Schema** — PostgreSQL table definition with typed columns, indexes, and inline comments.
3. **Tag Vocabulary** — categories, example tags per category, and hierarchy sketches.
4. **Entity and Relationship Model** — entity tables, predicates, and example rows (omit this section if your domain does not need it, and say why).
5. **Ingestion Pipeline** — ordered steps from raw input to indexed item, with AI prompts for extraction and tagging where relevant.
6. **Query Router Logic** — how incoming queries are classified and routed across the four retrieval layers, with weighting appropriate to your domain.
7. **Open Questions and Assumptions** — decisions you had to assume defaults for, flagged for human review.
8. **Information Gaps** — topics where you have no authoritative knowledge and which require input from a human stakeholder.

## Style and Discipline

- Answer in concrete specifics. Abstract answers are failures of the interview.
- Do not pad. Do not restate the questions in your answers unless it adds clarity.
- Do not flatter yourself, the reader, or the process.
- If you catch yourself generating a generic answer that could apply to any application, stop and regenerate with specifics drawn from your actual domain.
- If a section of the interview reveals that your understanding of your own application is thin, say so. An honest gap is more useful than a confident fabrication.
- The goal is a search system designed for your application specifically, not a template. Every answer should reflect what makes your domain different from every other domain.
